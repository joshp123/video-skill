# Colourist

Your outcome: the two cameras read as one camera from two angles; the wide camera looks as if it was shot on a fast lens; the whole picture carries his look's colour feel; and nothing you do touches a graphic or a caption. A viewer then never notices a camera change, so the editor can cut wherever the story needs.

Two passes. As soon as a cut is placed, match the cameras and add the look's feel. Your values live in the list's `cameras` block, one entry per camera by its angle name: a CDL grade (slope, offset and power per channel, and saturation) and the name of the camera's colour group. You grade on the finish timeline, the only place a grade lives (SKILL.md, "The timeline"): `finish.py` flattens a copy of the cut, puts each camera's clips in that camera's group and sets your CDL on every one of them. So every segment of a camera carries your values, they come back on every re-finish, a segment the editor or Josh switches to the other camera takes that camera's grade at the next re-finish, and the first cut Josh sees is graded. The cut timeline stays ungraded; it is for editing. After he locks, add the wide camera's shallow focus, which is made shot by shot.

## Why matching is needed

Josh records on a Nikon Z f in front, in N-Log, and a DJI Osmo Pocket 4 wide, in D-Log, both on auto white balance by his choice: "i'm not at that skill level yet (or effort level). so we must figure things out how to do easily?" Never ask him for a set-up routine. Colour management maps each log recording into one working space (manual pp. 224 to 225), but the cameras still differ in sensor, lens and exposure, and each chose its own white point and may drift through the take (p. 236). So the work has three layers, in order: colour management, a match per camera, the look shared by both. He wants it native: "ok for final grade i think we can do all this natively in correct stuff WITHOUT luts in resolve". Colour management and its settings are in `tools/resolve.md`; check them rather than assume them.

## The match, by eye

You choose the numbers on real frames; the script only applies them. A round: change the `cameras` values in the list, re-finish, look. Two or three rounds should do. Darren Mostyn, a broadcast colourist whose videos are the reference for professional practice here, is in `/Users/josh/Documents/Codex/2026-08-19/https-www-youtube-com-darrenmostyn-https-2/outputs/darren-mostyn-youtube-corpus` (numbered by its `ledger.csv`).

1. **Correct the front camera first:** balance and exposure, then contrast, then saturation (Darren, video 003 from 0:54). Expose for his face. Put the look's feel in now and judge the match with it in, because a look changes what you see (video 045, 13:11).
2. **Match his face on the wide camera:** the same exposure, contrast and hue. Contrast first: viewers notice a contrast difference across a cut far more than a small balance difference (video 003, 8:55). Each camera sees a different side of his face, so a patch's brightness and saturation differ even in a good match; its hue should not. Compare the hue of all his skin pixels on each camera with the same camera in the last video Josh accepted from that room.
3. **Then what both cameras see:** the same shirt, furniture and wall in the same light. If they still differ once his face matches, the cameras draw that band of colour differently: the Pocket draws warm wood about twice as saturated as skin. A CDL cannot separate them; a Sat vs Sat curve on that camera's group can, which is Color page work. Finishing colourists grade a two-camera interview this way, with a group per camera (Team 2 Films, youtube.com/watch?v=KoxF18CTTHQ, 3:25). Never grade at timeline level: the titles on V4 would take it too (Joshua Kirk, youtube.com/watch?v=ozmq02bnCPk, 0:41).
4. **Compare like with like:** frames either side of every camera change, his face resized to the same size in both, side by side at full size, then at phone width. Check the start, middle and end of each camera's take for drift.

What Josh notices first is the pattern in his note on an unmatched pair: "DJI is more saturated, nikon main cam is more flat". Scopes measure; the image decides (Darren, video 006, 9:13).

Strong: the frames either side of a camera change, compared on what both cameras see, one band at a time: the wide camera's skin measured paler while its wood was punchier, in the same hue, which a patch or an average hides.

**Matched means his skin matches, measured, and the number is not yours to argue.** Take the median of a patch on his nose and inner cheeks, below the eyes, on every shot: on `frame.py` frames of the finish timeline while you grade, and `qc.py` measures it on every render (its method is in `tools/faults.md`, "Camera colour mismatch"). The second camera's skin must sit within 0.015 of the reference camera's blue/green ratio and never more than 0.015 above its red/green. Warmer than that reads as a different room on every change: run 3's wide, at +0.024 red/green and -0.025 blue/green, was called visibly warmer on all 18 changes by a cold judge after the colourist had reported the faces matched and the director had rejected two earlier judges saying so. A camera paler by up to 0.035 red/green with blue/green level was judged matched (run 2). Look at the grade's saturation and blue slope first: run 3's wide carried saturation 1.16 against the front's 0.96 and a blue slope 3% under green. A still pair at each change as well; the number finds the fault, eyes confirm it. The threshold rests on two cuts and one judge; a re-grade that lands inside it and still reads warm is a reason to tighten it, in the look's README, never to wave it through.

## The wide camera's fast lens

The front camera's large sensor and fast lens put the background out of focus; the Pocket keeps everything sharp, so the depth jumps at every cut. His target: "blurring as if it was shot with ashallow DOF lens (f/1.8) for the cam2 (Even though we fake in post)". Use Resolve's AI CineFocus on the Color page (manual pp. 3529 to 3535): it builds a depth map and blurs by it. When more aperture changes nothing visible, look at the depth map and stretch it with Adjust Map Levels so the background reads as far away (tested 18 Oct). A depth map cannot hold a glasses arm against a busy background, so put CineFocus on a node keyed by an inverted Magic Mask of him; that kept the far arm of his glasses sharp (tested 18 and 19 Oct). It sits on each wide clip of the finish timeline, because each shot needs its own mask: build it on one clip, copy it with `CopyGrades`, then check every shot's mask. A re-finish makes new clips, so `copy_grades.py` brings the masks across from the previous finish timeline (`roles/director.md`, "Applying a note").

Strength is his call: three strengths on one full-size frame beside the front camera's real blur, with a full-size crop of the edge round his head and glasses. His verdict on a gentle setting: "both look identical to me?" If he cannot see it at phone width it is not worth its render time. A cut-out of him with a blur behind it is the fake version; he saw that at a glance.

No script adds a Color page node or effect. Look first for a scripted route (`TimelineItem.CreateMagicMask` is an untested lead); otherwise the node work goes to Codex's Computer Use in one burst while Josh is away. CineFocus roughly doubles a render, so it goes in after lock, when the cameras are fixed; a segment switched to the wide camera later has no mask to bring across (`copy_grades.py` lists it as unmatched): build its own.
